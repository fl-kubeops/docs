[Heading 1] Abstract
[Normal] Conventional machine learning paradigms require centralizing sensitive patient data on a single server, a model that fundamentally conflicts with modern privacy, sovereignty, and security mandates in healthcare. While superficial solutions such as column-level deletion (anonymization) or transport encryption appear expedient, empirical studies demonstrate that de-identified datasets remain vulnerable to correlation and re-identification attacks (Sweeney, 2000; Netflix Prize case study). Moreover, encryption-at-rest or in-transit still necessitates decryption at the central server for model training, creating a single point of compromise and violating data-locality requirements under HIPAA, GDPR, and Pakistan's emerging National Health Data Policy.
[Normal] Federated Learning (FL) resolves this algorithmically by training models directly on end-user devices without raw data transmission; yet existing frameworks, Flower, OpenFL, FedScale, provide only research-grade algorithms, leaving a critical infrastructure vacuum: no production-ready orchestration, automated failure recovery, declarative configuration, or MLOps pipeline integration exists.
[Normal] FL-KubeOps addresses this gap by building a Kubernetes-native operator (kopf framework) and Kubeflow-integrated MLOps pipeline specifically designed for privacy-preserving federated learning across distributed healthcare nodes. The system architecture comprises three co-designed layers: (1) a Client Layer of simulated hospital nodes (Docker containers on K3s) performing local training on private data; (2) a Server/Orchestration Layer managing round scheduling, model broadcast, aggregation, and failure recovery via a custom FLCluster CRD; and (3) a Security Layer employing PySyft Secure Multiparty Computation (SMPC) to ensure that individual client gradients are mathematically irretrievable from aggregated results, even at the central server.
[Normal] The system automatically detects and recovers from node failures within 60 seconds, tracks every training round in MLflow, and deploys on commodity hardware via a single declarative YAML manifest. By reducing FL deployment from weeks of manual scripting to minutes of infrastructure-as-code, FL-KubeOps demonstrates that privacy-preserving machine learning can be accessible, reproducible, and enterprise-ready without ever exposing a patient's private data in decipherable form.
[Heading 1] Introduction and Background
[Heading 2] Product Vision & Problem Context
[Normal] Product Description:
[Normal] Healthcare institutions generate the richest machine-learning training data available, patient records, laboratory results, diagnostic imaging metadata, and clinical notes. Yet regulations such as the U.S. Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), and Pakistan's emerging National Health Data Policy mandate strict data locality: patient information must not leave the facility of origin in any decipherable form. A hospital in Lahore cannot legally transmit patient records to a shared server in Islamabad, even to train a model that would benefit all participating institutions. Consequently, each hospital trains clinical AI models in isolation, producing statistically weak models that fail to generalize across population diversity, a critical deficiency in a country like Pakistan where disease phenotypes vary significantly across provinces.
[Normal] A common objection, raised during the Idea Defence, is that this problem could be solved through simpler means: deleting columns containing personally identifiable information (PII) or applying column-level encryption before transmission. While these approaches present superficial appeal, empirical studies demonstrate their inadequacy for healthcare AI. First, removing sensitive columns (diagnoses, lab values, demographic markers) strips the very features that carry the highest predictive signal for clinical models; a diabetes-prediction model cannot function without glucose readings. Second, correlation attacks and re-identification remain potent threats: Sweeney (2000) famously demonstrated that 87% of the U.S. population could be uniquely identified using only zip code, gender, and date of birth. In Pakistan's close-knit demographic contexts, quasi-identifiers such as age, gender, caste, and admission date are highly distinctive. The 2013 Washington State hospital discharge data breach proved that even "de-identified" datasets can be re-identified by linking auxiliary public records. Third, encryption-at-rest or in-transit still requires decryption at the central server for gradient computation, creating a single point of compromise and violating the principle of data sovereignty. Fourth, differential privacy noise injection, while mathematically rigorous, degrades model utility in low-sample clinical settings; homomorphic encryption, while secure, is orders of magnitude slower than federated learning and infeasible for deep neural networks on commodity hardware.
[Normal] The regulatory requirement is not merely that data be "hard to read" during transit, but that data sovereignty be preserved, data must never leave the facility of origin in a form that permits reconstruction of individual records. Federated Learning (McMahan et al., 2017) satisfies this requirement by construction: only model gradients (mathematical weight updates) traverse the network, never raw patient records. However, the algorithm alone is not deployable. No production-ready infrastructure exists to orchestrate real FL deployments: Kubernetes has no native concept of a federated training round; Kubeflow Pipelines assumes centralized data access; and existing FL frameworks (Flower, OpenFL) provide client-server communication libraries but treat Kubernetes as a passive deployment environment rather than an orchestration primitive. This structural gap, an algorithm without an operating system, forces hospital IT teams to manually manage client lifecycles, handle mid-round node failures, coordinate aggregation timing, and track model versions: engineering overhead that no clinical institution can sustainably absorb.
[Normal] Literacy Survey:
[Normal] Federated Learning was introduced by McMahan et al. (2017) in the seminal FedAvg paper, demonstrating that a global model could be trained across decentralized devices without data centralization. Bonawitz et al. (2017) extended this with practical Secure Aggregation, using pairwise masks and threshold secret sharing to ensure that the server learns only the aggregated sum of client updates, not individual contributions. Since then, the field has expanded into differential privacy (Abadi et al., 2016), system-level frameworks (Li et al., 2020), and domain-specific healthcare applications (Rieke et al., 2020).
[Normal] On the infrastructure side, Flower (Beutel et al., 2020) and OpenFL (Reina et al., 2021) have emerged as the dominant open-source FL frameworks. Flower provides a flexible Python client-server API with pluggable aggregation strategies; OpenFL offers a peer-to-peer topology with Intel-optimized secure aggregation. However, both frameworks are fundamentally research tools. An analysis of open GitHub issues (as of July 2026) reveals recurring requests for Kubernetes operator integration, automated node recovery, and pipeline orchestration, features that remain unaddressed in their core roadmaps. Kubeflow (Google, 2024) provides mature MLOps pipeline primitives (TFJob, PyTorchJob) but assumes centralized data sources and offers no first-class support for federated round scheduling or cross-node gradient aggregation. Kubernetes Operators (Dobies & Wood, 2021) have proven effective for complex stateful workloads (databases, message queues), yet no operator exists that understands the FL lifecycle natively, the way TFJob understands distributed TensorFlow.
[Normal] The gap is therefore not algorithmic but infrastructural: existing tools provide the FL algorithm, but no production orchestration layer that automates deployment, recovery, versioning, and compliance logging at hospital scale. FL-KubeOps fills this vacuum by combining the cryptographic rigor of PySyft's SMPC with the declarative power of Kubernetes Custom Resource Definitions (CRDs) and the reproducibility of Kubeflow Pipelines.

[Normal] Proposed Solution:
[Normal] FL-KubeOps is a Kubernetes-native operator and Kubeflow-integrated MLOps pipeline designed specifically for federated learning orchestration among dispersed healthcare nodes. A hospital IT team defines an FLCluster custom resource, a single declarative manifest that specifies participating client nodes, aggregation strategy (FedAvg, FedProx), model architecture, and security parameters. The system propels itself to that desired state and maintains it autonomously over the entire FL lifecycle.
[Normal] The architecture comprises three integrated layers that are co-designed and mutually dependent:
[List Paragraph] Client Layer: Simulated hospital nodes run as Docker containers orchestrated by K3s (lightweight Kubernetes). Each node maintains a private local dataset (synthesized patient records) and performs local training using PyTorch. Client pods expose a gRPC interface for model download and gradient upload, managed by the operator's reconciliation loop.
[List Paragraph] Server/Orchestration Layer: A kopf-based Kubernetes operator watches FLCluster resources and executes the federated lifecycle: broadcasting the global model, collecting encrypted client updates, triggering aggregation, incrementing round counters, and persisting results to MLflow. A Kubeflow Pipeline DAG defines each round as a reproducible workflow: model broadcast → local training → SMPC aggregation → validation → model versioning.
[List Paragraph] Security Layer: PySyft's Secure Multiparty Computation (SMPC) primitives operate across the client-server boundary. Before transmission, client gradients are split into secret shares using Shamir's secret sharing; the aggregation server receives only masked shares and computes the aggregated result without ever possessing sufficient information to reconstruct any individual client's contribution. This guarantee holds even if the aggregation server is compromised.
[Normal] Removing any layer collapses the system: without the Client Layer, there is no data locality and no privacy guarantee; without the Server/Orchestration Layer, there is no global model convergence, no failure recovery, and no reproducible pipeline; without the Security Layer, gradients could be intercepted or inspected, violating the project's core privacy promise. Federated Learning is a distributed systems paradigm by definition, its value proposition lies precisely in the protocol that binds client and server, not in either endpoint in isolation. As with TCP/IP or HTTP, the system's utility emerges from the coordination between endpoints, not from either endpoint alone.

[Normal] Objective of the System
[Normal] The system is designed to achieve five quantifiable outcomes:
[List Paragraph] Kubernetes-Native Operator: Develop a kopf-based FLCluster Custom Resource Definition (CRD) with a reconciliation loop that manages the complete federated lifecycle (create, update, delete) on a live K3s cluster without manual intervention.
[List Paragraph] Reproducible MLOps Pipeline: Construct a Kubeflow Pipeline DAG that executes federated training rounds (model broadcast → local training → SMPC aggregation → validation) with all metrics and model versions tracked in MLflow.
[List Paragraph] Cryptographically Secure Aggregation: Integrate PySyft SMPC to guarantee that individual client model updates are mathematically irretrievable from the aggregated result, verified by inspection of intermediate data at the aggregation server.
[List Paragraph] Automate Fault Recovery: Implement automatic detection and recovery from simulated client pod failures mid-round, with recovery time not exceeding 60 seconds from failure onset.
[List Paragraph] Clinical Benchmark Validation: Achieve at least 85% of centralized model accuracy on a distributed patient-records benchmark across a minimum of four simulated hospital nodes, with communication overhead per round documented and reproducible.
[Normal] User Research
[Normal] Primary Research: Structured Interview with Industry Practitioner
[Normal] A structured, semi-directive interview was conducted on 15 July 2026 with a Senior Machine Learning Engineer at Arbisoft, Lahore. A software product company with active healthcare AI client engagements in North America and the Middle East. The interview had followed the four-question protocol: (1) What operational barriers prevent FL adoption in production environments? (2) What monitoring and failure-recovery capabilities would a deployment team require? (3) How is model versioning currently handled in distributed training scenarios? (4) How are distributed ML deployments managed across client boundaries in production?
[Normal] Key Findings:
[List Paragraph] Declarative Deployment Abstraction: The interviewee emphasized that current FL frameworks require "weeks of Python scripting just to handle pod restarts and model checkpointing." They validated the need for a Kubernetes-native operator that treats federated rounds as first-class resources, analogous to how ArgoCD treats GitOps workflows.
[List Paragraph] Observability Gap: Standard monitoring tools (Prometheus, Grafana) lack awareness of federated round state. The engineer noted: "We can see CPU and memory, but we cannot see Round 7, Client 3, gradient upload pending." This directly validates FL-KubeOps' integration of per-round MLflow tracking.
[List Paragraph] Model Versioning Complexity: In distributed training, model version drift across nodes is a critical issue. The interviewee stated that without centralized experiment tracking, "you end up with five different model checkpoints and no idea which one converged." MLflow integration addresses this explicitly.
[List Paragraph] On-Call Burden: Manual failure handling in distributed training requires 24/7 on-call engineering time that hospital IT departments cannot allocate. Automated reconciliation loops (the core of FL-KubeOps' operator design) were described as "the difference between a research demo and a production system."
[Normal] Expected Benefits:
[List Paragraph] Operational Efficiency: Reduces FL deployment time from weeks of manual scripting per hospital to minutes via a single “kubectl apply -f flcluster.yaml” command. Beneficiaries: Hospital IT Departments, MLOps Engineers.
[List Paragraph] Regulatory Compliance: Enables compliance with HIPAA, GDPR, and Pakistan's National Health Data Policy by ensuring no raw patient data traverses network boundaries. Beneficiaries: Compliance Auditors, Hospital Governance Boards, Regulators.
[List Paragraph] Clinical Model Utility: Achieves ≥85% of centralized model accuracy by pooling population-scale diversity without data centralization, producing models that generalize across demographic segments. Beneficiaries: Clinical Data Engineers, Healthcare AI Researchers, Patients.
[List Paragraph] Infrastructure Democratization: Packages production-grade FL expertise into declarative infrastructure, lowering the barrier to entry for institutions without specialized distributed-systems teams. Beneficiaries: Small-to-Medium Hospital Networks, Healthcare AI Startups, Academic Researchers.
[List Paragraph] Fault Tolerance: Automatic recovery from node failures (including load-shedding-induced reboots common in Pakistan) ensures training continuity without 24/7 on-call engineering. Beneficiaries: System Administrators, Hospital IT Staff.

[Heading 1] Non-functional Requirements
[Heading 2] Reliability
[List Paragraph] NFR-R01: The kopf-based operator reconciliation loop shall execute continuously with a watch stream timeout recovery mechanism, ensuring no missed cluster state transitions.
[List Paragraph] NFR-R02: Automatic recovery from a simulated client pod failure shall complete within 60 seconds from failure detection, including pod rescheduling, volume reattachment, and round-state re-synchronization.
[List Paragraph] NFR-R03: The system shall support graceful degradation: if a client node drops mid-round and quorum is maintained (≥3 of 4 nodes), aggregation proceeds without aborting the round.
[List Paragraph] NFR-R04: All operator state transitions shall be idempotent; applying the same FLCluster manifest twice shall not create duplicate resources or corrupt round counters.
[Heading 2] Performance
[List Paragraph] NFR-P01: Aggregation latency per federated round (model broadcast → local training → SMPC aggregation → validation) shall not exceed 30 seconds for 4 simulated client nodes on commodity hardware (8 GB RAM, 4-core CPU).
[List Paragraph] NFR-P02: Kubeflow Pipeline DAG execution overhead (scheduling, artifact passing, step initialization) shall not exceed 10% of total round time.
[List Paragraph] NFR-P03: The MLflow dashboard shall load experiment lists within 3 seconds for up to 100 logged rounds.
[List Paragraph] NFR-P04: gRPC control-plane communication latency between operator and client pods shall remain below 100 ms on a local K3s cluster.
[Heading 2] Use-ability
[List Paragraph] NFR-U01: A complete FL cluster shall be deployable via a single declarative YAML manifest  without requiring manual pod creation or script execution.
[List Paragraph] NFR-U02: The Kubeflow Dashboard shall be accessible via standard browser (Chrome, Firefox) without additional plugins, showing pipeline run status, step logs, and artifact links.
[List Paragraph] NFR-U03: The MLflow UI shall present per-round metrics (accuracy, loss, communication overhead) in sortable, filterable tables with downloadable CSV export.
[List Paragraph] NFR-U04: All error messages from the operator and pipeline components shall include actionable remediation steps (e.g., "Client pod failed liveness probe: check ConfigMap dataset-path").
[Heading 2] Other Non-functional Requirements
[Heading 3] Security Requirements
[List Paragraph] NFR-S01 (Data Sovereignty): No raw patient data shall traverse network boundaries at any point during training. Only cryptographically masked gradient shares (SMPC) may leave the client node.
[List Paragraph] NFR-S02 (SMPC Irretrievability): Secure Multiparty Computation shall guarantee that individual gradient contributions are mathematically irretrievable from aggregated results, verified by memory inspection and formal protocol compliance.
[List Paragraph] NFR-S03 (Transport Security): All control-plane communication (gRPC, Kubernetes API, MLflow tracking) shall use TLS 1.3 with certificates rotated every 90 days.
[List Paragraph] NFR-S04 (Audit Logging): Every model broadcast, gradient upload, aggregation event, and RBAC access decision shall be logged with UTC timestamp, actor ID, and action outcome to an immutable append-only log.
[List Paragraph] NFR-S05 (Secret Management): SMPC seed values and JWT signing keys shall be stored in Kubernetes Secrets, never in ConfigMaps or container images.
[Heading 3] Scalability Requirements
[List Paragraph] NFR-SC01: The FLCluster CRD shall support horizontal scaling from 4 to N client nodes without modification to the operator codebase, via replica count adjustment in the manifest.
[List Paragraph] NFR-SC02: The aggregation server shall support pluggable aggregation strategies (FedAvg, FedProx, custom) via a strategy interface, allowing future extension without pipeline recompilation.
[Heading 3] Explainability & Validation Requirements
[List Paragraph] NFR-E01: Every training round shall produce reproducible metrics logged to MLflow, including random seed, dataset hash, model architecture version, and hyperparameter snapshot.
[List Paragraph] NFR-E02: Kubeflow Pipeline DAG definitions shall be version-controlled and executable without code changes on any K3s cluster matching the documented version constraints.
[List Paragraph] NFR-E03: A third-party evaluator shall be able to reproduce the complete deployment and achieve equivalent benchmark results by following the published GitHub guide without team assistance.
[Heading 3] Compliance Requirements
[List Paragraph] NFR-C01 (HIPAA/GDPR Data Locality): The system shall enforce data locality by architectural design, client nodes must be co-located with data; the operator shall reject configurations where dataset paths reference network storage.
[List Paragraph] NFR-C02 (Audit Trails for Regulatory Inspection): Compliance auditors shall be able to export a complete, tamper-evident audit trail of all federated rounds (participant nodes, timestamps, SMPC verification hashes) in PDF format suitable for regulatory submission.

Term | Definition
Federated Learning (FL) | A distributed machine learning paradigm where models are trained across decentralized devices holding local data samples, without exchanging raw data.
Secure Multiparty Computation (SMPC) | A cryptographic protocol allowing multiple parties to jointly compute a function over their inputs while keeping those inputs private.
Kubernetes Operator | A method of packaging, deploying, and managing a Kubernetes application using custom resources and controller logic.
Custom Resource Definition (CRD) | A Kubernetes API extension that defines a new object type (e.g., FLCluster) stored in the etcd database.
Kopf | A Python framework for building Kubernetes operators using decorators and asynchronous event handling.
Kubeflow Pipeline | A platform for building and deploying portable, scalable machine learning workflows based on Docker containers and Kubernetes.
K3s | A lightweight, certified Kubernetes distribution designed for resource-constrained environments and edge computing.
MLflow | An open-source platform for managing the end-to-end machine learning lifecycle, including experiment tracking and model versioning.
PySyft | An open-source framework by OpenMined for privacy-preserving AI, providing SMPC, differential privacy, and federated learning primitives.
FLCluster | The custom resource defined by FL-KubeOps representing a federated learning deployment (clients, server, strategy, and security parameters).
Gradient Aggregation | The process of combining model updates (gradients) from multiple clients into a single global update.
Data Sovereignty | The principle that data is subject to the laws and governance structures of the nation or facility where it is physically located.
Differential Privacy | A mathematical framework for sharing information about a dataset by describing patterns of groups while withholding information about individuals.
Homomorphic Encryption | A form of encryption permitting computation on ciphertexts, generating encrypted results that decrypt to match plaintext computation results.
Re-identification Attack | A technique where anonymized datasets are linked with auxiliary data to re-identify specific individuals.
MLOps | A set of practices for collaboration and communication between data scientists and operations professionals to manage production ML lifecycles.
DAG (Directed Acyclic Graph) | A graph structure used in pipeline frameworks where nodes represent tasks and edges represent dependencies, with no cyclic dependencies.
Reconciliation Loop | The core control mechanism of a Kubernetes operator that continuously compares desired state (CRD spec) with observed state (cluster reality) and acts to converge them.
